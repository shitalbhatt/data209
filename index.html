Course description

Exploratory data analysis is the critical first step in analysing data from an experiment. EDA means exploring the data to see what it seems to say — looking beneath the surface for insight using both numerical and graphical techniques, before any hypothesis is imposed on it.

You cannot draw reliable conclusions from a large quantity of data by gleaning over it. It has to be examined carefully and methodically through an analytical lens. Thorough analysis helps detect mistakes, debunk assumptions and understand relationships between key variables — which eventually informs the choice of an effective predictive model.

This course takes a hands-on approach to building a foundation in the concepts, techniques and tools for exploring and preparing data for model building, using datasets and case studies from insurance, retail, pharma and healthcare.

Module 1 · CO1 Week 1

L1-2 — The role of EDA, and robust statistics

Topics covered

  • Role of EDA in the data science lifecycle
  • Exploratory vs. confirmatory analysis
  • Sampling bias and inference intuition
  • Distribution behaviour: skewness, kurtosis, outliers
  • Summary and robust statistics — median, IQR

Lecture notes

What EDA is for

Exploratory data analysis is the practice of looking at what the data seems to say before any hypothesis is imposed on it. Tukey named the field in 1977 as a deliberate counterweight to a statistical culture that had become almost entirely confirmatory. His argument was that the analyst's first duty is detective work — finding what is there — and that this work has its own methods, standards and failure modes.

The practical case is simpler. You cannot draw reliable conclusions from a large quantity of data by gleaning over it. Raw data is skewed, contains impossible values, and hides its own collection history. A model fitted to unexamined data inherits every one of those defects and reports them with false confidence.

Exploratory versus confirmatory
ExploratoryConfirmatory
Questionopen — what is going on here?fixed before the data is seen
Hypothesisan outputan input
Methodgraphical, iterative, scepticalformal tests, error rates
Successa surprising, checkable claima defensible accept/reject decision

The cardinal error is switching modes silently: exploring until something looks significant, then reporting it as though the hypothesis came first. That is p-hacking, and it is the reason EDA findings are stated as hypotheses for future testing rather than as results.

Sampling and what you may claim

Every statistic is an estimate from a window onto a population. Four ways that window distorts:

  • Selection bias — the sampling mechanism favours certain units. Website analytics only observe

people who reached the website.

  • Survivorship bias — failures are absent. Loan data holds approved applicants; rejected ones

are invisible.

  • Non-response bias — those who did not answer differ systematically from those who did.
  • Measurement bias — the instrument distorts. A sensor drifts; a zero means "not recorded".

Larger samples reduce variance, not bias. A biased frame stays biased at any size. Before generalising, ask the question that settles most disputes: who could have been in this dataset but is not?

Summary statistics: position, dispersion, shape

Three families of summary, and the discipline of pairing them correctly.

  • Position — mean (balance point), median (splits the data in half), mode (most frequent).

The mean is not wrong on skewed data; it answers a different question. The size of the mean–median gap is the diagnostic.

  • Dispersion — range, variance, standard deviation, IQR. The first three are computed around

the mean and inherit its sensitivity to extremes. The IQR is the width of the middle 50% and ignores the tails entirely.

  • Shape — skewness measures horizontal asymmetry; kurtosis measures tail weight.
Report mean with standard deviation, or median with IQR. Never mix a mean with an IQR.

On pima.csv, Insulin has skew +2.27; its mean is 155.5 against a median of 125.0. Both numbers are correct. Only one describes a typical patient.

Reading a boxplot

The box spans Q1 to Q3 with the median — not the mean — inside it. Whiskers extend to the furthest point within 1.5 × IQR of the box edges; points beyond are flagged, not condemned. Box position within the whiskers reveals skew: if Q3 − Q2 exceeds Q2 − Q1, the distribution leans right. A boxplot hides multimodality completely, so always pair it with a histogram or density plot.

L3-4 — Data types and representation

Topics covered

  • Nominal, ordinal, interval and ratio scales
  • Discrete vs. continuous
  • Structured vs. semi-structured vs. unstructured data
  • Text representation basics
  • Time-series behaviour: trend and seasonality

Lecture notes

Measurement scale decides the statistic

The dtype pandas infers is a storage decision. The measurement scale is a semantic one, and it governs which operations are meaningful.

ScalePropertyLegalIllegal
Nominalnames, no ordercounts, mode, chi-squaremean, median, ordering
Ordinalordered, unequal gapsmedian, percentiles, rank testsmean (debatable), differences
Intervalequal gaps, arbitrary zeromean, standard deviation, differencesratios
Ratioequal gaps, true zeroeverything, including "twice as much"—

An integer column can be nominal. In the shoppers dataset OperatingSystems, Browser, Region and TrafficType are stored as integers and are pure codes; averaging them produces a number with no referent. This is the single most common error in submitted assignments.

Independently of scale, variables are discrete (countable — page views, pregnancies) or continuous (any value in a range — BMI, duration). Discrete versus continuous decides the plot; the measurement scale decides the statistic.

Structure
  • Structured — fixed schema, rows and columns. CSV, SQL, spreadsheets. Directly analysable.
  • Semi-structured — self-describing but irregular. JSON, XML, logs. Needs flattening first.
  • Unstructured — no tabular form. Text, images, audio, video. Requires a representation

decision before any analysis at all.

Storage format matters too: CSV carries no dtypes and breaks on delimiters inside text; Excel silently coerces types and hides rows; Parquet is typed and columnar but not human-readable.

Text becomes countable

Raw text is human-readable and machine-useless. Exploration begins after a representation choice:

  1. Tokenise — split into words; normalise case, strip punctuation, optionally drop stop words.
  2. Bag-of-words — count each vocabulary term per document. Interpretable; ignores word order.
  3. TF-IDF — down-weight terms common across all documents, up-weight distinctive ones.
  4. N-grams capture short phrases; embeddings capture meaning — both cost interpretability.

For exploratory purposes, document-length distribution, vocabulary size and ranked term frequency are usually sufficient. A ranked frequency bar chart is precise; a word cloud is decorative.

Time series

Four components are present in every series: trend (long-run direction), seasonality (a repeating pattern of fixed period), cyclic behaviour (long waves of no fixed period) and the irregular residual.

Two rules follow, and both matter for the final project: with time-ordered data you may not shuffle rows, and you may not split randomly. Training on rows recorded after the test rows is temporal leakage, covered properly in L29-30.

An ordered Categorical for a month column is not cosmetic. Sorted alphabetically, a seasonal pattern becomes invisible; sorted chronologically, the same data shows it immediately.

L5-6 — Unstructured data and ethics

Topics covered

  • Image as a matrix; audio as a waveform
  • Video as a sequence of frames
  • Curse of dimensionality — introduction
  • Data bias and ethical concerns

Lecture notes

Everything becomes an array
  • Image — a grid of pixel values. Grayscale is (height, width); colour is

(height, width, 3). Explore the intensity histogram: exposure, contrast, and clipping at 0 or 255 tell you what was lost at capture and cannot be recovered.

  • Audio — amplitude sampled over time: a 1-D array plus a sample rate. Explore the

waveform for structure, the amplitude histogram for symmetry, and short-term energy for loudness over time. Silence is often the majority of a field recording.

  • Video — a sequence of frames, (frames, height, width, channels) plus a frame rate. Each

frame is an image, so image technique applies frame by frame.

Once represented as an array, the ordinary exploratory questions apply unchanged: what is the distribution, where are the extremes, what is missing, and is anything clipped or saturated?

The curse of dimensionality — a flag planted

As dimensions grow, data density collapses exponentially. In a unit cube, the proportion of volume lying within 0.1 of an edge is 20% in one dimension, 49% in three, and 99% in twenty — almost every point is near a boundary. Distances between points converge, so "nearest neighbour" stops carrying information, and distance-based methods (k-NN, k-means) degrade first.

This is introduced here only. The response — feature selection and dimensionality reduction — is Module 4.

Bias enters before you write any code

Four kinds, all present before analysis begins:

  • Historical bias — the world the data records was already unequal. Faithful data can encode

unfair outcomes.

  • Representation bias — some groups are under-sampled; findings will not transfer to them.
  • Measurement bias — the proxy is not the thing. Arrests are not crime; clicks are not interest.
  • Aggregation bias — one model for everyone hides subgroup differences. Simpson's paradox is

the statistical form of this, and it appears again in L11-12.

An ethics checklist

Run this before analysing, not after:

  1. Who is in this dataset, who is missing, and would they have consented to this use?
  2. Does it contain personal or sensitive attributes, and do you actually need them?
  3. Could a finding here be used to disadvantage a group represented in the data?
  4. Are proxy variables standing in for protected attributes?
  5. What claim would be unsupportable outside the sampling frame — and have you written it down?

The Pima dataset records women of Pima heritage aged 21 and over. Every conclusion drawn from it is conditional on that frame, and a model trained on it must not be presented as a general screener.

L7-8 — Univariate EDA

Topics covered

  • Numerical variables: boxplot, density, distribution comparison
  • Categorical variables: frequency tables, bar plots
  • Detecting skewness and anomalies

Lecture notes

Two families, used in order

Non-graphical technique produces numerical summaries — quantiles, moments, frequency and contingency tables, correlation matrices. It is precise, reproducible and easy to tabulate, and it is blind to shape. Anscombe's quartet is four datasets sharing means, variances and correlation coefficients while looking completely different.

Graphical technique reveals shape, gaps, clusters and outliers instantly, and is sensitive to choices you make — bin width, axis limits, smoothing bandwidth.

Use both, in this order: compute the summary, plot it, then reconcile the two when they disagree.

The four questions

Answer all four for every column before looking at a single relationship.

  1. What is the centre? Mean or median, whichever the skew justifies. State which you used.
  2. What is the spread? Standard deviation or IQR, plus the range — which exposes impossible values.
  3. What is the shape? Symmetric, skewed, bimodal, uniform, zero-inflated. This decides everything downstream.
  4. What is unusual? Outliers, gaps, spikes at round numbers, impossible values.
Choosing a distribution plot
PlotShowsWeakness
Histogramfrequency per binbin width changes the story
Density (KDE)smoothed shapebandwidth invents smoothness; spills past data limits
Boxplotfive-number summaryhides multimodality completely
Violindensity plus summarycluttered with many groups
ECDFcumulative proportionless intuitive to a general audience

Default to histogram plus boxplot. Add a density overlay when comparing groups; reach for the ECDF when bin width is contested, since it requires no binning choice at all.

Reading anomalies off a plot
  • A spike at exactly zero usually means "not recorded", not "measured as zero".
  • Two humps mean two populations are mixed — find the variable that separates them.
  • A hard wall at a round number suggests a cap, truncation or data-entry limit.
  • Spikes at multiples of 5 or 10 indicate self-reported or rounded data.
  • A long thin tail is skew; isolated far points are candidate outliers. Different problems.

In pima.csv this reading finds 374 zeros in Insulin and 227 in SkinThickness — every one a disguised missing value that isna() cannot see. Module 3 treats them; Module 2 finds them.

Categorical univariate

Frequency tables with counts and proportions. Check cardinality (5 levels is a bar plot, 500 is a grouping problem) and imbalance. The shoppers Revenue flag is roughly 84.5% / 15.5%; the Pima outcome is 65% / 35%. Record this now — imbalance discovered here changes your sampling and evaluation strategy in Module 4, where a model predicting "never" would score 84.5% accuracy.

Topics covered

  • Correlation and covariance
  • Scatterplots and what they hide
  • Relationships between categorical variables

Lecture notes

Covariance and correlation

Covariance is the average product of paired deviations from the two means. Its sign gives the direction; its magnitude is uninterpretable because it carries the units of both variables. Changing metres to centimetres changes the number without changing the relationship.

Correlation is covariance divided by the two standard deviations: bounded in [−1, 1], unitless, and therefore comparable across pairs. That comparability is the entire point.

Choosing a coefficient
CoefficientMeasuresUse whenRobust
Pearson rlinear associationboth continuous, roughly linearno
Spearman ρmonotonic association on ranksnon-linear but consistently increasing; ordinalyes
Kendall τconcordance of pairssmall samples; many tiesyes
Cramér's Vassociation between categoricalstwo nominal variablesn/a
Correlation ratio ηcategorical vs numericgroup means differ across a categorypartly

Comparing Pearson with Spearman is a cheap non-linearity test: a large gap means the relationship is monotonic but not straight.

Correlation is not causation, and not shape

Correlation measures co-movement. It says nothing about which variable moved first, or whether a third moved both. And it says nothing about shape — hence the rule:

A correlation coefficient reported without its scatterplot is an unverified claim.

Five patterns worth naming when you look: linear, non-linear (Pearson understates it), heteroscedastic (spread widens along the axis), clustered (a single correlation over all points is meaningless — split first), and outlier-driven (a few points create or destroy the association; recompute without them and compare).

Relationships involving categories
  • Numeric vs categorical — grouped boxplots or violins; compare medians and spreads, not just

means. Quantify with a group-means table.

  • Categorical vs categorical — cross-tabulation with row or column percentages, never raw

counts. Quantify with chi-square, then Cramér's V for strength.

Percentages beat counts: 20 purchases from 40 sessions beats 100 from 1,000, and raw counts hide that. Always report the denominator alongside the rate.

With 12,330 rows almost every chi-square test returns a significant p-value. Read the effect size, not the p-value.

From pattern to hypothesis

A hypothesis names a mechanism, predicts a direction, and states what would falsify it.

  • Weak: "Glucose is related to the outcome." This restates the correlation and predicts nothing.
  • Better: "Elevated fasting glucose precedes diagnosis, so glucose will separate the classes better

than BMI — falsified if the separation vanishes after conditioning on age."

Name the confounders you cannot rule out with this data. Age confounds almost everything in the Pima dataset; the promotional calendar is unobserved in the shoppers dataset.

L11-12 — Multivariate structure

Topics covered

  • Multicollinearity and the variance inflation factor
  • Identifying groups in data
  • K-means for structure discovery
  • Interpreting clusters

Lecture notes

Why go past two variables

A relationship that holds within every subgroup can reverse when the subgroups are pooled. That is Simpson's paradox, and it is the strongest argument for multivariate analysis: two variables at a time can be consistently, confidently wrong.

Multicollinearity

Multicollinearity is strong correlation among the predictors themselves, not with the target. Its consequences:

  • Coefficient estimates become unstable — small data changes swing them, sometimes flipping sign.
  • Standard errors inflate, so genuinely useful variables appear insignificant.
  • Predictive accuracy may be unharmed; interpretability is destroyed.

It is invisible in a target-correlation ranking. You must inspect the predictor-to-predictor block of the correlation matrix, then confirm with the variance inflation factor.

VIF for a predictor is 1 / (1 − R²) from regressing that predictor on all the others. It answers: how redundant is this column?

VIFReadingAction
≈ 1uncorrelated with the otherskeep
1 – 5moderatekeep, note it
5 – 10coefficients becoming unreliableinvestigate the pair
> 10largely reconstructable from the othersdrop, combine, or reduce

In the shoppers data, BounceRates and ExitRates measure nearly the same thing. Keep one, or combine them — a decision revisited in L25-26.

K-means, exploratorily

Four steps: choose k and initialise (k-means++ rather than at random); assign each point to its nearest centroid; update each centroid to the mean of its members; repeat until assignments stop changing.

Scale the features first. K-means minimises Euclidean distance, so an unscaled variable with a large range decides the clusters by itself.
Choosing k, and being honest about the answer

The elbow on the inertia curve and the mean silhouette are the two standard aids. Silhouette compares each point's distance to its own cluster against the nearest other cluster, running from −1 to +1:

ScoreReading
> 0.7strong, well-separated
0.5 – 0.7reasonable structure
0.25 – 0.5weak; clusters overlap
< 0.25no substantial structure

On standardised pima.csv the best score across k = 2…6 is about 0.197. The correct conclusion is that this dataset has no strong natural grouping — and reporting that honestly is a good answer. Manufacturing clusters that are not there is not.

Interpreting clusters

A cluster is only real if you can describe it. Profile each one by computing every feature's mean within it and comparing to the overall mean; name it in domain language ("older, higher BMI, high glucose"), not as "cluster 2". Check sizes — a cluster of four points is an outlier group, not a segment. Re-run with a different seed and a different k: structure that vanishes was never there.

The grammar of graphics

A plot is built from layers. Thinking in layers is what lets you answer "which plot?" by reasoning rather than by recall.

  1. Data — the tidy table being shown, one row per observation.
  2. Aesthetics — the mapping from variables to visual properties: x, y, colour, size, shape.
  3. Geometries — the marks that carry the data: points, bars, lines, boxes, tiles.
  4. Scales — how data values become visual values: axis limits, log scales, colour palettes.
  5. Facets — small multiples; the same plot repeated across the levels of a variable.
  6. Coordinates and theme — coordinate system plus the non-data ink: labels, grid, legend.

Six ways a plot misleads

  • Dual axes — two scales on one chart manufacture correlation by choice of range.
  • 3-D and area encodings — doubling a radius quadruples the perceived size.

Non-numeric data

L17-18 — Missing data

Topics covered

  • MCAR, MAR, MNAR
  • Detection methods including disguised missingness
  • Imputation: mean/median, KNN, regression-based

Lecture notes

Missingness is data

The pattern of what is absent often carries more information than the values would have. Before imputing anything, ask why the value is missing — the answer determines whether any imputation is legitimate.

Three mechanisms
  • MCAR — missing completely at random. Absence is unrelated to anything. A tube was dropped.

Deletion is unbiased but wasteful; imputation is safe.

  • MAR — missing at random. Absence depends on observed variables. Older patients skipped a

test. Imputation conditioned on those variables works.

  • MNAR — missing not at random. Absence depends on the unobserved value itself. High earners

hide income. No imputation is unbiased; the missingness must be modelled.

You cannot prove MCAR, nor distinguish MAR from MNAR, from the data alone. You argue for a mechanism from domain knowledge.

Evidence you can gather

Create a binary indicator for missingness in each column and explore it like any other variable. Correlate it with the observed columns — a strong relationship argues against MCAR. Compare the distribution of other variables between the missing and non-missing groups. Visualise the missingness matrix: block patterns reveal structural causes such as a form change. Little's test gives formal evidence against MCAR, but not a MAR/MNAR distinction.

Best of all: ask the data owner why the field is blank. This beats every statistical test.

Keep the indicator. It costs one column, preserves the information you are about to overwrite, and documents your assumption.

Disguised missingness

pima.csv reports zero nulls. Yet 374 records (48.7%) have Insulin recorded as 0, and 227 (29.6%) have SkinThickness as 0 — and a living person has neither. Sentinel values defeat every automatic null check. This is the single most important idea in the module.

The menu, and its costs

What imputation costs

Mean imputation gives every missing row an identical value, so those rows contribute zero deviation from the mean and the standard deviation must fall. In the controlled experiment in P17-18 — where values are deleted with the truth retained — mean imputation lands 1,094 units from the truth on average and shrinks the standard deviation by 6.5%; KNN lands 517 units away and shrinks it by 1.2%. The dataset looks more precise than it is.

Rules

Fit the imputer on the training data only, after the split, inside a pipeline. Compare distributions before and after and report the change in mean, standard deviation and skew. Try at least two strategies. State the assumed mechanism in writing: "median imputation, assuming MAR given Age and BMI" is a complete claim.

L19-20 — Outliers

Topics covered

  • Z-score and IQR methods
  • Influence of outliers on modelling
  • When an outlier is an error and when it is the finding

Lecture notes

What an outlier is

A point far from the bulk of the data by some stated rule — a flag raised by a threshold you chose. Sometimes it is an error: a typo, a unit slip, a sentinel. Sometimes it is the most valuable record in the dataset. Fraud detection, fault diagnosis and rare-disease screening are fields where the outliers are the entire point.

An outlier is not automatically wrong and not automatically deletable. "Outlier" without a stated threshold is not a claim.

Univariate rules
MethodRuleAssumesFails when
Z-score\x − mean\/ SD > 3roughly normaloutliers inflate the SD and mask themselves
Modified z (MAD)0.6745·\x − median\/ MAD > 3.5symmetricheavily skewed data
IQR fenceoutside Q1 − 1.5·IQR or Q3 + 1.5·IQRnothingskewed data — over-flags the tail
Percentilebeyond the 1st or 99thnothingalways flags 2%, whether or not any exist

Masking is the trap: with two extreme values present, the standard deviation grows enough that neither exceeds 3. Use the MAD version, which is built on the median.

On raw pima.csv the IQR fence flags 34 Insulin points and 29 DiabetesPedigreeFunction points — the two most skewed columns. The fence flags the tail of a skewed distribution by construction. That is a signal to transform, not to delete.

Multivariate detection

A 1.9 m height and a 45 kg weight are each unremarkable; together they are worth checking.

  • Mahalanobis distance — distance from the centre accounting for covariance.
  • Isolation Forest — random splits isolate anomalies in fewer cuts; scales well.
  • Local Outlier Factor — compares local density to neighbours'; finds region-relative outliers.
  • Elliptic Envelope — fits a robust Gaussian; good when the data really is elliptical.
Influence

Outliers move the mean and inflate the standard deviation. They dominate squared-error objectives, so linear regression, k-means and PCA all chase them. High-leverage points can rotate a fitted line on their own. They defeat min–max scaling by stretching the range. Medians, IQRs, rank correlations and tree-based methods are comparatively unbothered.

The decision

Investigate first — always. Check against domain limits and the source record; most decisions become obvious once you know what the value is. Then:

  • Remove only when demonstrably an error. Record the count and the reason.
  • Treat — winsorise, cap at a percentile, or transform. Keeps the row, limits the influence.
  • Keep when it is a genuine extreme. Report it and use robust methods.

The settling question is empirical: recompute your headline statistic with and without the flagged points. If it barely moves, stop worrying about them. If it moves a lot, you have found something that needs explaining. Silent deletion is the failure mode.

L21-22 — Encoding strategies

Topics covered

  • One-hot, label and target encoding
  • Handling imbalanced and multi-class encoding
  • Dummy variables and the dummy variable trap

Lecture notes

Why encoding is a modelling decision

Most algorithms require numeric input, and the encoding you choose decides what the model can learn. Assigning 1, 2, 3 to unordered categories invents an order and a spacing that do not exist; the model will treat category 3 as "more" than 1 and as equidistant from 2.

For genuinely ordinal data, an ordered integer mapping is correct. For nominal data with few levels, one-hot is the safe default.

The schemes
SchemeWhat it doesUse whenRisk
Labelone integer per levelordinal data, or tree modelsinvents order on nominal data
Ordinal (mapped)integers in a stated ordertrue ordinal, order knownassumes equal spacing
One-hotone binary column per levelnominal, low cardinalitycolumn explosion; dummy trap
Binary / hashingcompact numeric encodinghigh cardinalitycollisions; loses interpretability
Frequencyreplace level with its sharehigh cardinality, frequency meaningfulunrelated levels share a value
Target / meanreplace level with mean targethigh cardinality, predictive levelsleakage

Start with one-hot. Move to something cleverer only when cardinality forces you, and then be explicit about the risk.

Two traps

The dummy variable trap. One-hot on k levels produces k perfectly collinear columns — any one is exactly one minus the sum of the others. Linear models cannot invert the resulting matrix, so coefficients become unstable or undefined. Fix: drop one level as the reference (drop_first=True). Trees and regularised models are unaffected, but the redundancy remains.

Target encoding leakage. Replacing a level with its mean target uses the answer to build the feature. Computed on the full dataset, every row sees its own target: validation looks excellent and live performance collapses. Fix: compute the mapping inside cross-validation folds, on training data only, and add smoothing so rare levels are pulled toward the global mean.

If a feature seems implausibly predictive, suspect leakage before celebrating.
Harder cases

High cardinality — group rare levels into "Other" by a stated frequency threshold before encoding. Keep the threshold as a parameter and report it; "levels under 1% of rows" is reproducible. Unseen levels at prediction time break a naive encoder — set handle_unknown='ignore'.

Imbalance changes evaluation, not encoding, but note it here. At 84.5% / 15.5%, accuracy is not an honest metric: report precision, recall, F1 and PR-AUC, and choose by which error costs more. Stratify every split and every cross-validation fold. Resampling — oversampling, undersampling, SMOTE — belongs inside the training fold only; applied before the split it duplicates minority rows across train and test, which is leakage. Class weights are often simpler and safer.

Multi-class targets — check that every class survives the split and that rare classes have enough rows to learn from.

L25-26 — Feature engineering and selection

Topics covered

  • Feature construction; interaction and polynomial features
  • Curse of dimensionality
  • Feature selection: filter, wrapper, embedded

Lecture notes

A feature is a question

Feature engineering is where domain knowledge enters the pipeline. It has the highest return on effort of any step and is the one most often skipped.

Six ways to build one:

  • Ratios — BMI from weight and height; conversion rate from clicks and views.
  • Differences — days since last visit; deviation from a group mean.
  • Aggregates — per-entity mean, max or count.
  • Date parts — month, weekday, is-weekend, hour. One date becomes several features.
  • Binning — age into clinical bands. Loses information, gains robustness.
  • Indicators — was_missing, is-zero, exceeds-threshold. Cheap, interpretable, often strong.

Every constructed feature needs a one-line rationale. If you cannot say why it should matter, do not build it.

Interactions and polynomials

Interaction features are products of two variables, capturing effects neither shows alone. Linear models cannot discover these; trees can, partially. Polynomial features are powers of a variable, letting a linear model fit a curve. PolynomialFeatures(degree=2) on 8 columns yields 44 — count before you generate. Both overfit quickly; pair with regularisation and validation.

Generate deliberately, not exhaustively. Automatic expansion is the fastest route into the curse of dimensionality.

Three families of selection
  • Filter — score each feature independently of any model (correlation, chi-square, mutual

information, variance). Fast, model-agnostic, blind to interactions.

  • Wrapper — search subsets by training a model on each (RFE, forward/backward selection).

Accurate for that model, expensive, overfits easily.

  • Embedded — selection happens during fitting: Lasso shrinks coefficients to zero, trees report

importances. A good default balance.

FilterWrapperEmbedded
Costvery lowhighmoderate
Model-specificnoyesyes

Run a filter first to remove the obviously useless, then an embedded method on what survives. Reserve wrappers for small feature sets.

Agreement is the signal

Applying five methods to the shoppers feature set, PageValues, has_page_value and ExitRates are chosen by all five; several are chosen by only one. A feature selected by one method alone deserves scrutiny.

The honest test of feature engineering is whether the constructed features survive selection — and whether a smaller set performs comparably. On the shoppers data, 18 features give cross-validated ROC-AUC 0.906 and a 6-feature consensus set gives 0.875. The smaller set is cheaper, more stable and easier to explain; whether the difference is worth it is a judgement you must state.

Select inside cross-validation. Selecting on the full dataset and then validating is leakage.

L27-28 — Dimensionality reduction

Topics covered

  • PCA — interpretation focused
  • LDA — the classification view
  • t-SNE and UMAP for visualisation only
  • Interpreting components; multicollinearity

Lecture notes

Selection versus reduction

Selection keeps a subset of the original columns; the output stays interpretable and the information in dropped columns is discarded. Reduction builds new columns as combinations of the originals; interpretation is harder but information from every column is retained.

Prefer selection when interpretability is the deliverable, reduction when variables are collinear or you need a 2-D view. Say which you did and why.

PCA in five steps
  1. Standardise — centre and scale. Skipping this makes PCA measure units, not structure.
  2. Covariance — compute the covariance matrix of the standardised features.
  3. Decompose — find eigenvectors (directions of variance) and eigenvalues (how much lies along each).
  4. Order and cut — sort by eigenvalue; keep as many as your variance target requires.
  5. Project — transform onto the retained components, each orthogonal to all the others.

PCA is unsupervised: it never looks at the target, so the direction of greatest variance need not be the useful direction.

Reading the output

Retention rules: a cumulative variance target (commonly 80–95%), the scree elbow, or the Kaiser rule (eigenvalue > 1). State which you used.

On the transformed shoppers features, PC1 explains 47.9% and four components reach 79.7% — a meaningful compression. On standardised pima.csv, the profile is 28.5 / 18.7 / 14.3 / 11.5 …, reaching 80% only at the fifth component. A flat scree plot is a finding, not a failure: it means the variables were already fairly independent.

Interpreting components

Read the loadings — the weight of each original variable in the component. A component with large weights on Glucose, Insulin and BMI is a "metabolic load" axis; name it. Signs matter: opposite signs mean the component contrasts two groups of variables.

A component you cannot name is a component you should not present to a domain audience.

Cautions: scale first; PCA is linear only; it is not appropriate for one-hot encoded categories, whose binary geometry breaks its assumptions; outliers distort components; and it ignores the target.

Multicollinearity detection with VIF

Reduction and multicollinearity are two responses to the same problem, so check for it before you reduce. The variance inflation factor for a predictor is 1 / (1 - R^2) from regressing that predictor on all the others: above 5 it is worth investigating, above 10 the column is largely reconstructable from the rest. (Introduced in L11-12; applied here as a screening step.)

Three responses, in increasing order of cost to interpretability:

  1. Drop one of the pair — cheapest, and defensible when the two measure the same thing.
  2. Combine them into a single constructed feature — keeps the information, keeps a name.
  3. Reduce with PCA — keeps all the information and produces orthogonal components by

construction, so multicollinearity is eliminated rather than managed. The cost is that the components need naming before anyone can act on them.

Note the asymmetry: PCA removes multicollinearity but does not tell you which original variable was redundant. If the deliverable is a recommendation about which measurements to keep collecting, use VIF and selection instead.

Lecture notes

Splitting

The test set exists to estimate performance on data the process has never seen. Touch it once.

  • Split before imputing, scaling, encoding, selecting or transforming — before everything.
  • Stratify on the target when classes are imbalanced.
  • Time-ordered data requires a time-based split: train on the past, test on the future, never shuffle.
  • Grouped data must be split by group, so all records for one entity sit on one side.
  • Set and record a random seed so the marker reproduces exactly what you saw.
Cross-validation

One split gives one noisy estimate. k-fold splits into k parts, training on k−1 and testing on the remainder, k times; k = 5 or 10 is standard. Stratified k-fold preserves class proportions and is the default for imbalanced classification. Time-series split uses expanding or rolling windows. Group k-fold keeps entities together.

Report the mean and the spread across folds. A high mean with a wide spread is an unstable result, not a good one.

Data leakage
Leakage is any path by which information from the test set — or from the future — reaches the training process.

It is the most expensive error in applied data science because it announces itself as excellent validation performance and only fails in production.

PathRiskFix
Preprocessing on all datascaler or imputer sees test statisticsfit inside a Pipeline, on train only
Target leakagea feature contains the answeraudit each feature's availability at prediction time
Temporal leakagetraining on rows recorded after test rowssplit by time; check both date ranges
Duplicate leakagethe same entity in train and testdeduplicate first, then split by group

The diagnostic question for every feature: would this value have been available, with this content, at the moment of prediction? A useful blunt check is single-feature AUC — anything above about 0.95 suggests the feature is standing in for the target.

Sampling skewed data — imbalanced targets

Identify the imbalance during EDA and state it. Accuracy is not honest at 85/15. Report precision, recall, F1 and PR-AUC, and choose by which error costs more. Stratify every split and every fold. Resampling belongs inside the training fold only; class weights are simpler and leak nothing.

Turning exploration into insight

The structure of a credible EDA report, and of your final project:

  1. What the data is — source, sampling frame, rows, columns, period; what it cannot speak to.
  2. What was wrong with it — the cleaning log, and every decision you made.
  3. What it shows — findings, each with the figure that supports it and a sentence of meaning.
  4. What comes next — hypotheses with falsification conditions, the modelling-ready dataset,

and the caveats.

A negative result, well established, is a contribution. "K-means finds no strong segmentation (silhouette 0.20)" is a finding worth reporting.

def univariate_sweep(frame, cols=None):
    cols = cols or frame.select_dtypes(include=[np.number]).columns.tolist()
    out = []
    for c in cols:
        s = frame[c].dropna()
        q1, q3 = s.quantile(0.25), s.quantile(0.75)
        iqr = q3 - q1
        out.append({"column": c, "mean": s.mean(), "median": s.median(),
                    "std": s.std(), "IQR": iqr, "skew": s.skew(),
                    "zeros_%": (s == 0).mean() * 100,
                    "iqr_out_%": (((s < q1 - 1.5*iqr) | (s > q3 + 1.5*iqr)).mean() * 100)})
    return pd.DataFrame(out).set_index("column")

Mean vs. median

The mean is the balance point of the distribution; the median is the value that splits it in half. They agree on symmetric data and diverge on skewed data. The size of the gap is the diagnostic: a mean far above the median means a long right tail pulling the average up.

# ---- Mean vs median ----------------------------------------------------
centre = pd.DataFrame({
    "mean"  : df[numeric_cols].mean(),
    "median": df[numeric_cols].median(),
})
centre["difference"]  = centre["mean"] - centre["median"]
# ratio guards against divide-by-zero on columns whose median is 0
centre["mean/median"] = np.where(centre["median"] != 0,
                                 centre["mean"] / centre["median"], np.nan)
centre = centre.sort_values("difference", ascending=False)
print(centre.to_string())

print("\nInterpretation")
print("- Where median = 0 the majority of sessions never touched that page type at all.")
print("- ProductRelated_Duration: mean far exceeds median -> strong right skew.")
print("- Report the median for these columns; the mean describes no typical session.")

Variance vs. IQR

Variance and standard deviation measure spread around the mean, so they inherit its sensitivity to extreme values. The IQR measures the width of the middle 50% and ignores the tails entirely.

Pair them correctly: mean with standard deviation, or median with IQR. Never mix.

# ---- Variance vs IQR ---------------------------------------------------
q1 = df[numeric_cols].quantile(0.25)
q3 = df[numeric_cols].quantile(0.75)

spread = pd.DataFrame({
    "variance": df[numeric_cols].var(),
    "std"     : df[numeric_cols].std(),
    "IQR"     : q3 - q1,
    "range"   : df[numeric_cols].max() - df[numeric_cols].min(),
})
spread["std/IQR"] = np.where(spread["IQR"] != 0, spread["std"] / spread["IQR"], np.nan)
print(spread.sort_values("std/IQR", ascending=False).to_string())

print("\nInterpretation")
print("- std/IQR far above 1 means the tails are inflating the standard deviation.")
print("- Those columns need a robust summary (median + IQR) and, later, a transformation.")

Skewness and distribution shape

Skewness quantifies horizontal asymmetry; kurtosis quantifies tail weight. A working rule for reading the numbers:

\skew\ShapeWhat to do
< 0.5roughly symmetricmean and standard deviation are fine
0.5 – 1.0moderately skewedprefer median; consider a mild transform
> 1.0strongly skeweduse median and IQR; transform before modelling
# ---- Skewness, kurtosis, shape classification --------------------------
def classify_shape(s):
    a = abs(s)
    if a < 0.5:  return "roughly symmetric"
    if a < 1.0:  return "moderately skewed"
    return "strongly skewed"

shape = pd.DataFrame({
    "skew"    : df[numeric_cols].skew(),
    "kurtosis": df[numeric_cols].kurtosis(),
    "zeros_%" : (df[numeric_cols] == 0).mean() * 100,
})
shape["direction"] = np.where(shape["skew"] > 0, "right tail", "left tail")
shape["shape"]     = shape["skew"].apply(classify_shape)
shape = shape.sort_values("skew", ascending=False)
print(shape.to_string())

strong = shape[shape["shape"] == "strongly skewed"].index.tolist()
print(f"\n{len(strong)} of {len(numeric_cols)} numeric columns are strongly skewed:")
print(" ", strong)
print("\nCarry this list to P23-24, where these columns are transformed.")

Histogram, boxplot and density

Three views of the same variable, each hiding something the others reveal:

  • Histogram — shows gaps and multiple peaks, but the story changes with bin width.
  • Boxplot — compact five-number summary, ideal for comparing groups; hides multimodality completely.
  • Density (KDE) — smooth shape, easy to overlay; the smoothness is invented by the bandwidth.

Always look at at least two.

Perform univariate EDA

A univariate sweep answers four questions for every column: what is the centre, what is the spread, what is the shape, and what is unusual? Write it once as a function and reuse it in every project including the final one.

# ---- A reusable univariate sweep ---------------------------------------
def univariate_sweep(frame, cols=None):
    """Centre, spread, shape and anomaly flags for every numeric column."""
    cols = cols or frame.select_dtypes(include=[np.number]).columns.tolist()
    out = []
    for c in cols:
        s   = frame[c].dropna()
        q1, q3 = s.quantile(0.25), s.quantile(0.75)
        iqr = q3 - q1
        out.append({
            "column"    : c,
            "n"         : len(s),
            "mean"      : s.mean(),
            "median"    : s.median(),
            "std"       : s.std(),
            "IQR"       : iqr,
            "skew"      : s.skew(),
            "kurtosis"  : s.kurtosis(),
            "zeros_%"   : (s == 0).mean() * 100,
            "iqr_out_%" : (((s < q1 - 1.5*iqr) | (s > q3 + 1.5*iqr)).mean() * 100),
        })
    return pd.DataFrame(out).set_index("column")

# ---- Chi-square and effect size ----------------------------------------
from scipy.stats import chi2_contingency

def cramers_v(frame, a, b):
    tab = pd.crosstab(frame[a], frame[b])
    chi2, p, dof, _ = chi2_contingency(tab)
    n = tab.values.sum()
    v = np.sqrt(chi2 / (n * (min(tab.shape) - 1)))
    return chi2, p, v

Silhouette score (basic)

Silhouette compares how close a point is to its own cluster versus the nearest other cluster. It runs from -1 to +1:

ScoreReading
> 0.7strong, well-separated clusters
0.5 – 0.7reasonable structure
0.25 – 0.5weak; the clusters overlap
< 0.25no substantial structure — say so

Reporting a weak score honestly is the correct answer. Manufacturing clusters that are not there is not.

A controlled experiment

The shoppers dataset has no missing values, which is an opportunity rather than a problem: we can delete values ourselves and keep the truth. That lets us measure imputation error directly, which is impossible on real missing data.

We inject two mechanisms:

  • MCAR — values removed completely at random.
  • MAR — values removed with a probability that depends on another observed column.

Compare with the real-world case in pima.csv, where zeros in Glucose, BloodPressure, SkinThickness, Insulin and BMI are disguised missing values that isna() cannot see.

Detect outliers

Three univariate rules and one multivariate method. They disagree, and the disagreement is informative — a point flagged by every method deserves investigation; a point flagged by one may be an artefact of that rule's assumptions.

# ---- Detection rules ----------------------------------------------------
def iqr_flags(s, k=1.5):
    q1, q3 = s.quantile(.25), s.quantile(.75)
    iqr = q3 - q1
    return (s < q1 - k * iqr) | (s > q3 + k * iqr)

def z_flags(s, t=3.0):
    sd = s.std()
    return (s - s.mean()).abs() / sd > t if sd else pd.Series(False, index=s.index)

def mad_flags(s, t=3.5):
    med = s.median()
    mad = (s - med).abs().median()
    if mad == 0:                       # fall back when more than half the values are identical
        mad = (s - med).abs().mean() or 1.0
        return 0.7979 * (s - med).abs() / mad > t
    return 0.6745 * (s - med).abs() / mad > t
